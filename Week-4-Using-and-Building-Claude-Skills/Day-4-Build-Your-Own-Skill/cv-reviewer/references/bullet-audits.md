# Bullet-Level Audits

Four quick, mechanical tests to run against every bullet during the Impact, Content Quality, and Professionalism scoring passes. These complement — don't replace — the XYZ formula (`resume-writing-guide.md`) and the verb bank (`action-verbs.md`); run all of them together per bullet, not in separate passes.

## 1. The So-What Test

Read the bullet and ask: if my only reaction is "okay... and?", it fails.

- "Attended team meetings and contributed to discussions" — so what?
- "Supported the development of AI solutions" — so what?
- "Was part of a research project" — so what?

There's no neutral content on a resume — every line either adds signal or dilutes the ones around it. A bullet that fails this test needs a rewrite (add the outcome) or should be cut, not left as filler.

## 2. The Numbers Audit (Harvard quantification standard)

For every bullet, check whether a number is available and used. If it's missing, ask the candidate directly rather than assuming there isn't one — there almost always is:

- **How many people?** (team size, students, clients, users)
- **How much money?** (budget, revenue, savings, prizes, funding)
- **How much time?** (deadline pressure, speed of delivery, duration)
- **What percentage?** (improvement, reduction, growth, accuracy)
- **What scale?** (number of products, lines of code, documents, events)
- **What rank?** (1st of X, top Y%, selected from Z applicants)

Defensible estimate ranges are fine ("roughly 10 teammates," "cut load time from ~3s to ~1s"). Made-up numbers are not — see the Credibility Audit below. If a bullet genuinely has no number hiding in it after asking, that's rare enough to be worth double-checking with the candidate before accepting it.

## 3. The Credibility Audit

Every claim on the resume must be defensible in a real interview. For every major claim, check:

- Can the candidate explain exactly how this was done?
- What was their specific contribution versus the team's?
- If asked "walk me through this," could they hold the floor for five minutes?
- Is the number something they actually measured or tracked, or is it a guess dressed up as a fact?

Flag anything that reads as inflated, vague, or unverifiable — not to accuse the candidate of dishonesty, but because an unverifiable claim is a liability the moment an interviewer probes it. This is the same boundary `cv-optimizer` enforces when rewriting ("could the candidate defend this sentence, unprompted, in an interview?") — a review that lets an indefensible claim through just sets up a worse rewrite later.

## 4. The Buzzword Audit

Flag and recommend replacing with a real, specific claim:

- "Results-driven," "highly motivated," "passionate about," "dynamic," "detail-oriented"
- "Leverage synergies," "spearheaded initiatives," "thought leader"
- "Familiar with," "exposure to," "basic knowledge of" (these actively undersell — if it's worth listing, say what was actually built or used with it)
- "Assisted in the development of," "played a key role in"
- "Strong communication skills" (show it through a bullet, don't claim it as a trait)

Every buzzword is a wasted word occupying space a concrete claim could use instead. This overlaps with `common-mistakes.md`'s buzzword-overuse entry — use that file's fuller list when scanning Skills/Summary sections; use this one when auditing bullets one at a time.
